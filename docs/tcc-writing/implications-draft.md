# Managerial Implications

This chapter presents the main managerial implications derived from the analysis of the Telex Soluções Auditivas case.

The objective is to translate the findings of the study into practical lessons for managers responsible for marketing, CRM, sales, customer service, store operations and service design.

The case suggests that improving commercial performance in a service-based organization depends not only on generating more leads, but on designing an integrated operational journey capable of transforming digital interest into scheduled appointments, attendance and sales opportunities.

---

# 1. Lead Generation Is Not Enough

One of the main managerial lessons of the Telex case is that lead generation alone does not guarantee business growth.

In many organizations, marketing efforts are concentrated on increasing the number of leads generated through digital campaigns. However, if the company does not have an efficient structure to process, qualify, route, contact and schedule those leads, part of the investment in acquisition may be lost.

The Telex case shows that the value of lead generation depends on what happens after the lead enters the funnel.

The commercial journey must be managed as a system:

```text id="q6s1dw"
Lead generation
   ↓
Lead reception
   ↓
Lead registration
   ↓
Lead routing
   ↓
Human contact
   ↓
Appointment
   ↓
Attendance
   ↓
Sales opportunity
```

The managerial implication is clear: companies should not evaluate marketing performance only by the number of leads generated. They must also monitor the operational capacity to transform leads into concrete commercial outcomes.

---

# 2. Customer Journey Must Be Managed End-to-End

The case reinforces the importance of managing the customer journey as an integrated process.

In the previous model, the journey was fragmented between marketing, call center and stores. Each area had a role, but the handoffs between areas created delays and friction.

The redesigned model reduced the number of unnecessary intermediaries and created a more direct flow between the customer and the store responsible for the service.

From a managerial perspective, the journey should not be managed as isolated departments.

Instead, managers should ask:

```text id="fccmjl"
Where does the customer enter?
Who receives the lead?
Who owns the next step?
How fast is the response?
Where can the lead get stuck?
Who is accountable for solving friction?
```

The implication is that journey management requires cross-functional coordination between marketing, CRM, automation, call center and stores.

---

# 3. Friction Reduction Can Increase Scale

The simplification of the lead entry process was associated with a significant increase in the number of leads.

The previous model required the customer to provide more information before advancing in the journey. The redesigned model reduced the initial effort and allowed the customer to enter through WhatsApp with fewer required fields.

This suggests that reducing entry friction may help increase the top of the funnel.

The simplified logic is:

```text id="l7z65s"
Less initial effort
   ↓
More people start the journey
   ↓
Higher lead volume
```

However, managers should understand that friction reduction is not automatically positive in every dimension.

When it becomes easier to enter the funnel, the company may also receive more low-intent contacts, ghost leads or people who do not respond after the first interaction.

Therefore, the managerial challenge is not only to reduce friction, but to reduce the right friction.

The company must balance:

```text id="rm1714"
Ease of entry
   +
Lead quality
   +
Operational capacity
   +
Qualification rules
```

---

# 4. Speed Is a Strategic Variable

The reduction in time until scheduling was one of the most important operational improvements in the case.

In the previous model, scheduling could take up to 48 hours. In the redesigned model, this time was reduced to up to 2 hours.

This change has strong managerial relevance.

When a lead enters the funnel, the customer is often at a moment of active interest. If the company takes too long to respond or schedule, the customer may lose interest, forget the interaction, seek another provider or postpone the decision.

The managerial implication is that speed should be treated as a key performance indicator.

Companies should monitor:

* time to first automated response;
* time to first human response;
* time from lead entry to appointment;
* time from appointment to attendance;
* percentage of leads contacted within SLA;
* leads lost due to lack of response.

In service-based businesses, speed is not only an operational metric. It is part of the customer experience.

---

# 5. Automated Response Is Not the Same as Human Service

The Telex case also shows the importance of distinguishing between automated response and human service.

The chatbot provides immediate response and reduces silence after the customer’s first contact. However, the bot does not replace the human interaction required to understand the customer’s hearing-related need, build trust, schedule the appointment and conduct the sales process.

Managers should separate these two indicators:

```text id="n6s4yf"
Automated response time
```

and

```text id="qlrnlk"
Human response time
```

A company may have immediate bot response and still have slow human follow-up.

Therefore, the managerial recommendation is to create separate SLAs for each stage:

| Stage                       | Suggested Control           |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Bot response                | Immediate                   |
| Store notification          | Automatic and traceable     |
| First human contact         | Within defined SLA          |
| Appointment scheduling      | Within defined maximum time |
| Follow-up after no response | Standardized sequence       |

This distinction prevents managers from overestimating the effectiveness of automation.

---

# 6. CRM Should Be Treated as an Operating System

One of the most important implications of the case is that CRM should not be treated only as a database.

In the redesigned model, CRM became part of the operational architecture of the journey. It supported registration, routing, ownership, follow-up and performance analysis.

The CRM flow connected:

```text id="acc0uf"
WhatsApp
   ↓
Bot
   ↓
RD Conversas
   ↓
RD CRM
   ↓
Google Sheets
   ↓
Pluga
   ↓
Store notification
   ↓
CSO action
```

This means that CRM must be managed as a process, not merely as software.

Managers should ensure that the CRM has clear rules for:

* lead status;
* lead ownership;
* responsible store;
* responsible CSO;
* source of the lead;
* first contact time;
* last contact time;
* appointment status;
* attendance status;
* sales status;
* reason for loss;
* next action.

Without this governance, the CRM may store information but fail to guide action.

---

# 7. Ownership Is Central to Lead Management

The shift of lead ownership from the call center to the store was a central change in the Telex case.

In the previous model, the call center controlled the initial lead journey and acted as an intermediary between customer and store.

In the redesigned model, the store became responsible for contacting, scheduling and following up with the customer.

This change has a strong managerial implication: every lead must have a clear owner.

A lead without an owner is at risk of being lost.

Ownership means that someone is responsible for:

```text id="bumqko"
contacting
qualifying
scheduling
confirming
following up
updating status
closing or losing the opportunity
```

The manager should be able to answer, at any time:

```text id="cjb1bp"
Who owns this lead?
What is the current status?
What is the next action?
When was the last interaction?
Why did the lead not progress?
```

If these questions cannot be answered, the lead management process is not mature enough.

---

# 8. Store-Level Autonomy Requires Store-Level Accountability

The redesigned model gave more autonomy to stores.

This autonomy can improve speed and proximity to the customer, because the store has better visibility of its own agenda, team availability and local context.

However, autonomy also requires accountability.

If stores receive leads but do not act quickly or do not update the CRM properly, the journey becomes fragmented again.

Therefore, managers should monitor performance by store.

Recommended store-level KPIs include:

* leads received;
* first human response time;
* contact rate;
* appointment rate;
* attendance rate;
* no-show rate;
* sales conversion rate;
* lost lead rate;
* average time to appointment;
* CRM update compliance.

The implication is that decentralization should not mean lack of control.

It should mean local responsibility supported by central visibility.

---

# 9. Call Center Should Focus on Exceptions and Complex Cases

The redesigned model repositioned the call center.

Instead of owning every lead, the call center became more focused on exceptions, such as:

* customers who had difficulty using the bot;
* customers who could not find the desired city;
* customers with complaints;
* customers with doubts outside the standard flow;
* cases requiring human support before routing.

This has an important managerial implication.

The call center should not necessarily be eliminated. It should be redesigned around the tasks where human support adds the most value.

In this model, the call center can act as:

```text id="o2sm1t"
exception handler
support layer
quality control point
fallback for automation
relationship recovery channel
```

This allows the company to combine automation with human support instead of choosing one over the other.

---

# 10. Automation Requires Governance

Automation increases speed and scale, but it also increases the need for governance.

When more leads enter the funnel, the company needs better control over statuses, ownership, follow-up, quality and exceptions.

Automation without governance can create hidden problems, such as:

* leads routed to the wrong store;
* duplicate records;
* leads without follow-up;
* stores overloaded with unqualified contacts;
* ghost leads counted as opportunities;
* incomplete CRM updates;
* lack of visibility about lost leads.

Therefore, automation should be accompanied by:

```text id="p7pmop"
clear business rules
standardized statuses
fallback paths
quality monitoring
human escalation
data validation
dashboard visibility
periodic process audits
```

Managers should not treat automation as a one-time implementation.

It should be treated as a system that requires continuous improvement.

---

# 11. Lead Quality Must Be Measured, Not Assumed

The case indicates that reducing friction increased lead volume but also created more low-quality or ghost leads.

This means that lead quality must be measured systematically.

Managers should not evaluate the funnel only by total leads.

Recommended lead quality indicators include:

* qualified lead rate;
* ghost lead rate;
* cold lead rate;
* hot lead rate;
* disqualified lead rate;
* contactability rate;
* response rate after store contact;
* appointment confirmation rate;
* reason for loss;
* reactivation rate.

A more mature lead quality model could classify leads as:

```text id="j7xw8w"
New
Qualified
Hot
Warm
Cold
Ghost
Lost
Disqualified
Reactivated
```

The managerial implication is that growth in lead volume must always be interpreted together with lead quality.

---

# 12. Customer Experience Begins Before the Appointment

In the Telex case, the customer experience begins before the customer arrives at the store.

The experience starts when the customer sees an ad, clicks, enters WhatsApp, interacts with the bot, waits for contact, receives a message from the store and schedules the appointment.

This is especially relevant in hearing care, where the customer may feel insecurity, resistance, fear, embarrassment or dependence on family support.

Managers should understand that the pre-appointment journey already shapes the customer’s perception of the brand.

A slow or confusing journey may reduce trust.

A fast, clear and humanized journey may increase confidence.

Therefore, pre-appointment experience should be managed with the same importance as in-store service.

---

# 13. Service Businesses Need Hybrid Journeys

The Telex case suggests that service businesses should not think in terms of automation versus human service.

The most effective model may be a hybrid journey.

In this hybrid model:

```text id="vdu4ua"
Automation handles:
entry, routing, basic data collection, notifications and scale.

Humans handle:
listening, trust, scheduling, objections, empathy, sales and post-sale support.
```

This is particularly important in health-related services, where the customer journey involves emotional, functional and relational dimensions.

The managerial implication is that automation should remove operational friction so that human teams can focus on the interactions where they add more value.

---

# 14. Data Visibility Is Essential for Decision-Making

The redesigned model creates more data points across the journey.

However, data only generates value if managers can use it to make decisions.

The company should create dashboards to monitor:

* lead entry by channel;
* lead volume by store;
* response time by store;
* appointment scheduling time;
* attendance rate;
* no-show rate;
* sales conversion;
* lead quality;
* lost reasons;
* SLA compliance;
* operational bottlenecks.

The objective is not only to report results, but to identify where the journey is breaking.

Managers should use data to answer:

```text id="k7bxuu"
Which stores respond faster?
Which stores lose more leads?
Which channels generate better leads?
Where do customers abandon the journey?
Which stage has the highest friction?
Which leads should be prioritized?
```

This turns CRM and funnel metrics into tools for continuous improvement.

# 15. Recommended Actions for Telex

Based on the case analysis, the following managerial actions are recommended.

---

## 15.1 Create a Formal Lead Governance Manual

The company should formalize rules for:

* lead classification;
* ownership;
* response time;
* contact attempts;
* appointment confirmation;
* no-show follow-up;
* loss reasons;
* reactivation;
* priority leads;
* CRM update standards.

---

## 15.2 Monitor Store-Level SLA

Each store should be monitored according to response and scheduling indicators.

Suggested KPIs:

* first human contact within SLA;
* average time to first human contact;
* average time to appointment;
* percentage of leads without action;
* delayed follow-up rate.

---

## 15.3 Separate Bot Metrics from Human Metrics

The company should report:

```text id="kzjo00"
bot response time
```

separately from:

```text id="sgdmhr"
human response time
```

This prevents artificial improvement in response indicators.

---

## 15.4 Improve Lead Quality Classification

The CRM should allow more precise classification of:

* ghost lead;
* cold lead;
* qualified lead;
* hot lead;
* lost lead;
* disqualified lead;
* reactivated lead.

This would allow better evaluation of lead quality after friction reduction.

---

## 15.5 Build a No-Show Recovery Flow

Because attendance is a critical stage, the company should create a standardized no-show recovery flow.

Example:

```text id="v6s0dd"
No-show identified
   ↓
Same-day WhatsApp message
   ↓
Next-day call attempt
   ↓
New appointment option
   ↓
Reason for no-show recorded
   ↓
Reactivation attempt after defined period
```

---

## 15.6 Create a Bot Escalation Protocol

The bot should have clear escalation triggers.

Examples:

* customer does not find city;
* customer sends complaint;
* customer expresses urgency;
* customer is elderly or dependent;
* customer asks for human support;
* customer repeats the same message;
* customer abandons before completion.

---

## 15.7 Audit CRM Data Quality Monthly

A monthly CRM audit should verify:

* leads without owner;
* leads without status;
* leads without next action;
* duplicated leads;
* leads without source;
* appointments without outcome;
* sales without funnel tracking;
* lost leads without reason.

---

## 15.8 Create Store Performance Dashboards

Dashboards should allow comparison between stores.

Suggested views:

* funnel by store;
* SLA by store;
* attendance by store;
* sales by store;
* lead quality by store;
* no-show by store;
* lost reasons by store.

---

## 15.9 Train CSOs on Journey Ownership

CSOs should be trained not only in sales, but in journey management.

Training should include:

* response speed;
* WhatsApp communication;
* active listening;
* CRM update discipline;
* appointment confirmation;
* no-show recovery;
* lead prioritization;
* customer trust building;
* post-sale follow-up.

---

## 15.10 Review Incentive Structures

The company should review whether incentives are aligned with the desired journey.

Incentives should avoid encouraging lead retention, poor qualification or superficial scheduling.

Possible incentive metrics:

* qualified appointment rate;
* attendance rate;
* conversion rate;
* CRM compliance;
* customer satisfaction;
* follow-up quality;
* reactivation success.

---

# 16. Recommended Actions for Similar Organizations

The Telex case also provides lessons for other B2C service organizations that depend on converting digital leads into physical or human-assisted appointments.

Recommended actions include:

```text id="l96cdb"
1. Reduce unnecessary entry barriers.
2. Respond immediately after customer intent.
3. Separate automated response from human response.
4. Define clear lead ownership.
5. Use CRM as an operational workflow.
6. Create routing rules.
7. Monitor response time and scheduling time.
8. Measure lead quality.
9. Maintain human fallback.
10. Manage the journey end-to-end.
```

These recommendations apply especially to businesses such as:

* healthcare clinics;
* dental clinics;
* audiology services;
* aesthetic clinics;
* education services;
* insurance brokers;
* financial advisory services;
* automotive dealerships;
* franchises;
* local service networks.

---

# 17. Risks to Monitor

The redesigned model should continue to be monitored because some risks may emerge over time.

Key risks include:

* overload of stores with low-quality leads;
* inconsistent CRM updates;
* lack of response from some units;
* overdependence on automation;
* customer frustration with the bot;
* loss of human warmth in the initial interaction;
* duplicated leads;
* inaccurate performance dashboards;
* lack of standardization between stores;
* misinterpretation of operational cost as CAC.

Managers should treat the new model as a continuous improvement system.

---

# 18. Strategic Summary

The main managerial implication of the Telex case is that customer journey redesign can be a strategic lever for conversion improvement.

However, the case also shows that technology alone is not enough.

The strongest gains appear when technology is combined with:

```text id="e3w6ff"
clear processes
CRM governance
defined ownership
fast human follow-up
store accountability
data visibility
continuous monitoring
```

The redesigned model changed the commercial architecture of Telex by reducing the dependency on centralized intermediation and bringing the customer closer to the store.

This allowed the organization to process more leads, reduce scheduling time and improve funnel continuity.

At the same time, it increased the importance of lead quality control, CRM discipline and local execution.

---

# 19. Final Managerial Takeaway

The Telex case suggests that service companies should not ask only:

```text id="cg9wy8"
How can we generate more leads?
```

They should also ask:

```text id="hjzj55"
How fast can we respond?
Who owns the lead?
How clearly is the next step defined?
Where does the customer experience friction?
How do we know whether the lead was properly followed up?
How do we balance automation and human service?
```

The managerial value of the case lies in showing that conversion is not only a marketing outcome.

It is the result of an integrated operating system that connects marketing, CRM, automation, people, process and customer experience.
